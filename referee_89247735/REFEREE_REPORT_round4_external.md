## New or continuing major issues

### 1. The +8.02-minute correction is reproducible but is not the target barycentric correction

**Location and claim:** p. 3, §3.2: “TESScut times carry a per-target barycentric correction”; S66/S93 differ by “+0.20 min ... throughout (a cadence-time convention difference).” These premises are also in `s104_clock_89247735.py`. [M,C]

**What is wrong.** TESScut inherits the FFI reference-position correction. The [Lightkurve spacecraft timing tutorial](https://lightkurve.github.io/lkspacecraft/tutorials/calculating-barycentric-time-correction.html) explicitly demonstrates this distinction and the need to recompute it at the target position. Thus QLP-minus-TESScut includes a sky-position-dependent correction difference. A pooled S66/S93 offset cannot be transplanted to S104 as a universal convention offset. My direct measurements are:

| Sector | QLP and cutout sample counts | QLP − TESScut TIME, minimum / median / maximum (min) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 66 | 12154 / 12154 | +0.133844 / +0.194742 / +0.246931 |
| 93 | 11047 / 11047 | +0.164177 / +0.206075 / +0.240282 |
| 104 | 11227 / 11227 | −8.085683 / −7.562195 / −6.654938 |

These come directly from [C]. I checked **every adjacent cadence increment**, comparing QLP `CADENCENO` increments to `round(diff(TESScut TIME−TIMECORR)*86400/200)`: zero mismatches in all three sectors. This removes the 200-s alias ambiguity. The pooled control is +0.201361 min; interpolation at the script's BTJD 4196.8043 gives −7.821028 min, reproducing its +8.022389 min. But the S104 QLP time differs from `TESScut TIME−TIMECORR` by only **−0.08510188 min = −5.1061 s** throughout: it has effectively lost the positional barycentric term. [C]

I then queried [JPL Horizons](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api), with `COMMAND='-95'` (TESS), `CENTER='500@0'`, `EPHEM_TYPE='VECTORS'`, `REF_PLANE='FRAME'`, `REF_SYSTEM='ICRF'`, `OUT_UNITS='AU-D'`, `VEC_TABLE='2'`, and `TLIST` equal to the matched `TIME−TIMECORR+2457000`. At each event I projected the returned spacecraft position onto the unit vector at RA 275.223692413°, Dec −45.5611672045°, using `dot(r,n)/c`. This is a separate physical calculation, not another QLP/TESScut nearest-neighbour comparison. The results near each fitted event are:

Retaining that QLP convention instead of adjusting all four events calls for approximately **+7.53314 min** in S104. The manuscript's +8.02239 min is consequently about **29–31 s too large**, depending on the convention. ### 2. Correct the neighbour dilution arithmetic and the overcorrection of the eclipse-shape argument

**Location:** abstract p. 1; §4.2 pp. 5–6: “37–40% eclipse,” “a non-grazing eclipse ... can be flat-bottomed,” and “remains a viable ... scenario.” [M]

**What is wrong.** §3.3 and Table 1 explicitly define QLP SAP depths as **deblended to the target**. The neighbour's G-band flux ratio is `10**(-0.4*3.635611)=0.0351363` relative to the target, or about 0.02765 relative to the adopted total aperture. If the observed QLP depth is δQLP, the aperture depth under this model is `0.787*δQLP`. Therefore the neighbour eclipse depth is

`δEB = (0.787*δQLP)/(0.787*0.0351363) = δQLP/0.0351363`,

namely **28.46–31.31%**, for the quoted 1.0–1.1% depths, under the manuscript's G-as-TESS flux approximation. Dividing δQLP directly by the aperture fraction gives the erroneous 36–40%. A definitive TESS-band number requires a colour/response/aperture model; 28–31% is the corrected arithmetic under the supplied approximation, not an independently measured EB eclipse depth. The neighbour's different colour makes that distinction consequential. [M,G]

The geometry also needs more care than either the revised manuscript or the round-3 report supplies. For an ordinary dark-body, full transit in the approximately constant-velocity limit,

`q = T23/T14 = sqrt(((1-k)^2-b^2)/((1+k)^2-b^2))`.

With the nominal fitted `q=3.654940/4.536242=0.805720`, this implies `k <= (1-q)/(1+q) ≈ 0.1076`; the corresponding uniform-brightness blocked area is at most approximately 1.16%, before modest limb-darkening effects. A large dark disk producing a 30% eclipse cannot simultaneously produce this long flat bottom in that simple configuration. Stellar density sets the overall duration but cancels from this approximate duration ratio. These values are calculated from [F], not inferred from a plot.

**Why it matters.** A flat bottom alone does not exclude a deep eclipse, but the *fractional flat-bottom duration* is substantive evidence. Conversely, fitting a target-hosted dark transit is not a complete test of luminous binaries, total occultations, eccentric configurations, or blended triples. Neither “excluded” nor “demonstrated viable” follows from the present discussion. The paragraph in §5 p. 7 asserting that “a fainter unresolved star cannot host it” also needs its transit-model assumptions stated; it is not a universal exclusion of all unresolved EB configurations. [M,F]

**Required fix.** Correct the normalization and identify the bandpass approximation; replace the argument with the actual conditional shape constraint. For a candidate note it is sufficient to say that neighbour-hosted eclipses have **not been comprehensively ruled out**, that simple deep dark-body transits are strongly constrained by the shape, and that no general EB model has been fitted. A stronger claim of physical viability/exclusion needs an explicit diluted EB fit to the same data. The round-3 referee's dilution calculation and dismissal of the duration ratio were incomplete; the authors should not preserve those errors merely to comply with that review.

## Checks performed and limits

### Detection, aliases and fit

I directly read the raw FITS and reproduced the four-event batman fit; the transit morphology and the nominal host-density consistency are credible. Under an independent missing-transit test requiring at least 70% of a four-hour transit covered by quality-zero samples, plus local out-of-transit baseline coverage, `P/n` for n=2–12 predicts respectively **5,6,15,17,21,29,34,32,42,44,48** additional usable windows. Different coverage criteria explain why these counts differ from the manuscript's 4–43; I do not call its count wrong without matching its exact rule. ## Publication assessment and minimum additional work

**RNAAS:** A corrected, much shorter note reporting a repeatable transit-like candidate and a useful ephemeris is a reasonable potential contribution without claiming validation. The current submission is not in that format: it has ten pages, two figures and two tables. [M] The [RNAAS preparation rules](https://journals.aas.org/research-note-preparation-guidelines) limit notes to 1500 words including the specified ancillary text, a 150-word abstract, and one figure **or** table; RNAAS is editor-moderated, not peer reviewed. For that route, the minimum is the target-specific timing correction, consistent predictions and numerical audit, corrected dilution/shape statements, clear conditional radius and unconstrained mass, accurate catalog/credit statements, and retrievable essential products. A calibrated neighbour exclusion or new RV detection is not necessary if the note makes no such claim.

**AJ / a short letter:** The AAS journal lineup distinguishes AJ from **ApJ Letters**; “AJ letter” should not be treated as a separate validation standard. [AAS journal descriptions](https://journals.aas.org/astronomical-journal/), [AAS manuscript preparation](https://journals.aas.org/manuscript-preparation/) A short AJ candidate paper must still establish a worthwhile, reproducible scientific result. In my judgment this single-object paper would be materially strengthened by a resolved ground-based transit, or by calibrated target-versus-neighbour pixel likelihoods plus explicit diluted-EB modelling and a controlled dilution/radius analysis. RVs are required for a measured mass or planet/brown-dwarf classification, not merely to report an honest unvalidated candidate. The current work does not establish a confirmed planet, an excluded neighbour, or an upper mass bound. The minimum immediate revision is to correct what is already calculable and make the claimed result match those limits; additional observations should be required only for the stronger conclusions they would support.
