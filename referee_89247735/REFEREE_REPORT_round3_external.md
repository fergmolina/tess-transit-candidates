# External referee report — 2026-10-19 — GPT-6 Codex; sources opened: local `paper/main.pdf`, `paper/main.tex`, `paper/refs.bib`, the local QLP FITS and TESScut FITS for Sectors 13/66/93/104, analysis JSON/CSV and scripts under `results/` and `scripts/`; public GitHub API/tree/README at both supplied repositories; public Zenodo API metadata and the first supplied Zenodo archive; Gaia DR3 TAP and SIMBAD TAP; MAST QLP product description; cited literature pages/PDFs where listed below. The supplied DOI for the concurrent Zenodo record resolves to the record's current version, not its initial record ID.

## Recommendation: Major revision

The four dips are real, agree in depth and shape across the two Sector 93 events and other-sector events, and are compelling evidence for a repeatable approximately 18.323-day transit-like signal. The adjacent-sector pair strongly constrains the cycle count and the period is unlikely to be a simple submultiple alias. The transit geometry and the reported stellar density are also internally plausible. The manuscript nevertheless makes a central, demonstrably incorrect assertion about Sector 104 timing: the QLP timestamps for the event are short by the target's barycentric light-travel correction, about 7.5 minutes, despite the text claiming the authors recomputed that correction and found an error below one minute. This affects the event time, the fitted ephemeris, and the claimed extraction discrepancy. Separately, the pixel error calibration does not support the quoted 2.4-sigma neighbour exclusion; the nearby eclipsing-binary scenario is not ruled out by the stated secondary-eclipse argument; and the Gaia RV mass limits are not reproducibly derived from the published catalogue summary. The photometric candidate is worthy of further consideration, but the paper's present false-positive and companion-mass conclusions exceed its evidence. A revised timing reduction, calibrated localization comparison, and a transparent RV likelihood or suitably cautious wording are needed.

## Major issues

### 1. Sector 104 has an uncorrected barycentric timestamp offset (pp. 3, 7; §3.2; Table 1; §6)

**What is wrong.** The paper says the authors recomputed the QLP barycentric correction from FFI spacecraft times, found Sectors 66 and 93 correct to 0.03 min and Sector 104 correct to at most 1 min, and therefore attribute the 5–13 min QLP/TESScut timing difference to photometry. I compared the local QLP and TESScut files at the Sector 104 event, examined the TESScut `TIMECORR` column, recomputed the geocentric barycentric light-time for the target with Astropy, and checked that the QLP and TPF cadence-gap patterns agree. In S104, QLP `TIME` is about 7.76 min earlier than the spacecraft time before barycentric correction across the sector; the target correction is about 7.40 min. The QLP event time, BTJD 4196.8043, is about 7.48 min earlier than the corresponding corrected spacecraft time, BTJD about 4196.8095. Sector 66 and Sector 93 residuals are only about 0.03–0.05 min. The corrected Sector 104 epoch is also consistent with the earlier three times. A weighted fit using these illustrative corrected event times gives P about 18.323169 d and residuals about -0.25, +1.19, -0.33, -0.20 min; this is not a replacement published solution, because the exact FFI timestamp construction and photometric refit should be documented by the authors.

**Why it matters.** This contradicts the manuscript's timestamp validation and its explanation of the S104 offset as an extraction-dependent transit midpoint. The midpoint adopted in the v3 ephemeris, 4196.80850, happens to be close to the corrected event, but it is justified as an arbitrary midpoint between extractions rather than a verified barycentric time. The quoted 6-min systematic then conflates photometric extraction uncertainty with a known timestamp correction. The BJD times can still yield a good ephemeris after correction, but the present method, error budget, and UTC predictions are not trustworthy as presented.

**What would fix it.** Redo the cadence-level S104 timestamp conversion from the FFI spacecraft times using documented spacecraft position/velocity ephemeris, compare against the current QLP and TESScut `TIME`/`TIMECORR`, show the cadence matching and residuals for each event, and refit all event times/ephemeris with the corrected timestamps. Explain whether the displayed QLP v01 file is the affected version and whether a corrected MAST release exists. Propagate the corrected event-time uncertainty, including photometric extraction variation and timestamp uncertainty as separate terms. Regenerate the timing table and every prediction from the corrected fit.

The MAST QLP page confirms a barycentric timing-correction issue in Sectors 99–104 and says affected files were in the process of being updated when the page was accessed ([MAST QLP status](https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsp/qlp)). Its product description defines `TIME` as barycenter-corrected BTJD, `TIMESYS=TDB`, and the target-arrival reference as the Solar System barycenter ([QLP product description, §§2.3.2–2.3.3](https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsps/qlp/hlsp_qlp_tess_ffi_all_tess_v1_data-prod-desc.pdf)). For clarity, the local FITS show 28 flagged cadences in the ±2-h window and all 72 in-transit cadences flagged; the manuscript should state both counts explicitly (see Minor/factual checks).

### 2. The centroid result does not establish the claimed 2.4-sigma preference (pp. 4–5; §4.2; Figure 2)

**What is wrong.** The injected-window calibration reports a centroid RMS about the target position of 0.16, 0.17, 0.19 pixels, including a fixed target-image-centroid versus WCS-position offset of 0.12–0.17 pixels. The text then treats this full, target-centred radial RMS as an isotropic per-window measurement error and divides it by √2 for the two S93 events. That does not calibrate the uncertainty of the difference between the measured transit-loss centroid and each hypothesis. The calibration mixes a fixed WCS/image-centroid bias with random scatter; the two-event Sector 93 combination is divided by √2 without demonstrating that the injected errors are independent or centred; and no covariance or anisotropic error model is given. The empirical centroid error should be estimated from the distribution of recovered centroid *offsets about their mean*, then compared in a common image frame to the target and neighbour hypotheses with the fixed image-centroid offset treated consistently.

Using the manuscript's own saved centroid JSON, the published target and neighbour χ² values (4.0 and 9.7) reproduce, as does Δχ² = 5.75. But converting √Δχ² = 2.40 into “2.4σ” is not a calibrated significance. For two fixed hypotheses with specified Gaussian errors, a likelihood ratio is exp(Δχ²/2) ≈ 17.8, which is not a general-purpose Gaussian sigma and depends directly on the disputed covariance model. An illustrative scatter-only error estimated from the injected-window x/y scatters, with the fixed offset handled as a common bias, gives materially different per-sector separations and does not justify the paper's stated aggregate sigma. Figure 2's pixel-scale maps also have too few independent resolution elements to infer a small subpixel source offset from peak pixel significance alone.

**Why it matters.** The paper calls pixel localization a decisive test and uses it to disfavor the only resolved neighbour. The quoted significance is the main quantitative support for that conclusion. A flux-loss centroid within roughly 0.3 px of two sources separated by roughly 0.35 px cannot be assigned a calibrated probability from 60 injected windows unless the systematic offset and event-to-event covariance are modelled correctly.

**What would fix it.** Publish the per-injection centroid vectors, report their mean and full 2×2 covariance per sector, distinguish fixed image-centroid/WCS offsets from random uncertainty, and use an injection/recovery experiment under both target-hosted and neighbour-hosted transit hypotheses. Fit the pixel time series or difference images at the two known Gaia positions with the same nuisance background/PSF model and compare predictive likelihoods; calibrate the likelihood ratio by injections. Until then say the images are consistent with the target and do not significantly distinguish the 7–10″ neighbour. The concurrent analysis's independent difference-image offsets (+1.0±1.6″ E, +2.0±2.0″ N) are useful corroboration but do not themselves resolve a 9″ source at TESS resolution; its PSF-fit Δχ² is not a substitute for calibration ([concurrent report, commit 5537855](https://github.com/alejandrozarco/tess-candidates-2026/tree/5537855)).

### 3. The nearby background eclipsing binary is not excluded by the arguments given (pp. 4–5; §4.2)

**What is wrong.** The neighbour supplies about 2.75% of the quoted aperture light, so a 1.0–1.1% aperture dip requires an eclipse depth of roughly 36–40% on that source. A deep eclipse can have a flat bottom if the eclipse is non-grazing; the measured target-hosted value T23/T14=0.81 does not by itself rule out such an eclipsing binary, since the duration ratio also depends on impact parameter, radius ratio, and the neighbour's stellar density. More importantly, a dark or very faint secondary can produce a deep primary eclipse and a negligible secondary eclipse. The manuscript acknowledges “unless the companion is dark” but then treats the no-secondary limit as evidence excluding the neighbour. The 900-ppm aperture limit is not a generic lower bound on the secondary eclipse of every possible 37% primary-eclipse binary.

The referenced TRICERATOPS result is also described too categorically. The concurrent public record describes a range of seed results and states that the reported FPP is only known as an upper value near 3×10^-3; it identifies unresolved companion scenarios as the dominant remaining probability. The scenario table provided for the nominal run has a near-neighbour EB probability printed as zero after numerical underflow, while the target-hosted companion (including substellar companions) is not represented by those EB scenarios. A zero rounded/underflowed Monte Carlo scenario probability is not proof of physical impossibility.

**Why it matters.** A background binary is a standard astrophysical false positive for FFI photometry. The flux decrement may well be on the target, and the adjacent pair makes the periodicity persuasive, but the manuscript's own pixel result only disfavors the neighbour weakly and its eclipse-shape/secondary logic does not close the scenario space. The claim “what excludes it” is too strong.

**What would fix it.** Model explicit eclipsing-binary configurations on the neighbour using its Gaia subgiant parameters, dilution, primary and secondary depths, eccentricity, radius ratio, and the measured TESS-band transit shape; show which physical parameter space is excluded and which remains. If an EB with a faint secondary remains viable, retain the neighbour as an unresolved/weakly disfavored false-positive scenario and make a small-aperture ground-based transit or high-resolution imaging/spectroscopy the decisive test. Describe TRICERATOPS as conditional prioritization, not validation, and state its seed variability and scenario limitations.

### 4. The Gaia DR3 radial-velocity mass limit is not established by the calculation shown (pp. 6–7; §5)

**What is wrong.** Gaia DR3 gives this star a median RV of -21.618 km s^-1, `radial_velocity_error` 0.544 km s^-1, 14 transits, `rv_chisq_pvalue` 0.519, `rv_renormalised_gof` -0.165, and `rv_amplitude_robust` 5.677 km s^-1. Inverting the DR3 median-error relation to obtain an epoch-RV scatter of about 1.59 km s^-1 is reasonable as an approximation, but the manuscript does not specify a valid statistical model for the following step. It says “requiring no excess scatter” at p=0.05 gives K<2.1 km s^-1 and at p=0.01 gives K<2.6 km s^-1. The catalogue's p-value for constancy is a goodness-of-fit result for the observed epoch RVs, not a direct likelihood for a sinusoidal orbit with known phase coverage; the individual epoch RVs are unpublished. No derivation of the test statistic, treatment of the 0.11-km/s floor, phase sampling, uncertainty in the scatter, or conversion from a p-value threshold to an upper confidence bound is provided. Further, the manuscript later calls `rv_amplitude_robust` a 2K estimate; this is a robust observed range, not a calibrated upper limit on a Keplerian peak-to-peak amplitude.

Numerically, the stated K limits convert to about 21.7 and 27.0 MJup (not exactly 21 and 26 under the stated 0.69-Msun star and 18.323-day period); 5.677/2=2.84 km s^-1 converts to about 29.5 MJup. Those conversions are approximately sound, but the upper limits themselves are not supported by the published information. A simple idealized χ² calculation is sensitive to whether one conditions on the measured scatter or propagates the unknown noise variance; it does not uniquely recover the quoted 2.1/2.6 km s^-1.

**Why it matters.** The abstract and summary use the RV analysis to claim M2≲25–30 MJup and to rule out stellar companions/part of the brown-dwarf range. This is a central physical conclusion, yet Gaia does not publish the epoch RVs needed for a standard orbit fit.

**What would fix it.** Either obtain/fit independent RVs, or provide a fully specified Monte Carlo/injection calculation using Gaia's transit count, DR3 uncertainty model and RV-amplitude definition, with confidence-level coverage tested under null and injected circular/eccentric orbits and random Gaia sampling. If that cannot be done, report the DR3 observed robust range and p-value as qualitative evidence against a large-amplitude stellar binary, explicitly state that no formal mass upper limit follows from DR3 summary fields alone, and remove the numerical 25–30 MJup “bound.” The Gaia source fields were independently retrieved from Gaia DR3 TAP; the published RV error relation is in Katz et al. ([Gaia DR3 RV paper](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2023/06/aa44220-22.pdf)).

### 5. Transit shape and radius errors are conditional and need a dilution/stellar-prior sensitivity analysis (pp. 3, 5, 8; §3.3, Table 2, §5)

**What is right.** I independently recomputed the circular transit density from P=18.32317 d and a/R*=32.96: ρcirc≈1.43 ρ☉. The TIC mass/radius give ρ*=0.69/0.785^3≈1.43 ρ☉, and the stated T14/T23 values (4.52/3.66 h) are geometrically consistent with k≈0.098, b≈0.27, a/R*≈33. Rp≈8.37 R⊕ for R*=0.785 R☉ and Rp≈7.89 R⊕ for 0.74 R☉, so the reported rounded radii are arithmetically consistent.

**What remains problematic.** The fit fixes quadratic limb-darkening coefficients at (0.52, 0.18), fixes e=0, uses local linear baselines, and reports MCMC intervals without a sensitivity analysis to limb darkening, dilution, stellar density, eccentricity, or time-correlated noise. The TESS event data are 200-s cadence and the model is integrated, which is appropriate, but χ²=1554/1556 only demonstrates a good aggregate fit under the adopted per-point noise model; it does not show that the formal posterior intervals include correlated low-frequency baseline and extraction systematics. The amplitude/depth is particularly sensitive to the assumed deblending: the target's fraction of the aperture is 0.787 in this paper, while the concurrent r=2 px extraction reports 0.730–0.737 and, with free dilution and an informative prior, finds k=0.1075 and Rp=8.68 R⊕ for the SED radius. The paper says flux-fraction differences explain about one third of the k discrepancy, leaving the rest unexplained, but it does not present a common-extraction fit that resolves it. The transit density matches the TIC density partly because a circular orbit and fixed LD are imposed; this is not a test of eccentricity or a validation of the host assignment. The SED radius is imported from the concurrent analysis and is not independently derived in this manuscript.

**What would fix it.** Report posterior sensitivity fits with LD priors rather than fixed coefficients, with flux fraction/dilution either jointly fit or constrained by a documented Gaia/PSF model, and with reasonable baseline orders and red-noise treatment. Compare the same raw pixels/aperture and data selection with the concurrent solution. Propagate stellar radius and mass uncertainties explicitly; distinguish conditional transit-fit errors from systematics and stellar-parameter uncertainty. The cautious 7.9–8.4 R⊕ interval across the two radii is more defensible than a single precise “0.75 RJ” characterization.

### 6. The UTC prediction files and observability claims need correction (p. 7; §6; archived `*_utcfix.csv`)

**What is wrong.** The manuscript correctly states that the original “UTC” column was only a BJD_TDB-to-UTC scale conversion and omits the barycentric light-travel time, up to ±8 min. In the corrected CSV, the new `utc_geocentric` column fixes the mid-transit timestamps, but `ingress_utc` and `egress_utc` remain identical to the old scale-only conversion and are not shifted by the same light-time correction. For example, epoch 159 is listed at geocentric 2027-06-25 02:09 UTC, while the four-hour contacts are listed as 00:17 and 04:17; after subtracting the same +7.9-min light-time from the old UTC contacts they should be approximately 00:09 and 04:09 UTC. This is a factual error in the files, although the text only quotes midpoints.

The table's ephemeris uncertainty is only about 3.1 min at epoch 159 and 3.3 min at epoch 162 from the v3 fit, whereas the text uses a conservative ±15-min observing window. That is fine as an operational margin if labelled as such, but it is not the statistical 1σ prediction uncertainty. Also, the claimed first “full” Chile-observable transit at epoch 159 is close to the stated altitude cutoff: a direct site/solar-altitude calculation places the transit near the threshold at CTIO. The definition of “full” and observing constraints should be given.

**What would fix it.** Regenerate all UTC columns (midpoint and contacts) from the corrected BJD_TDB values and remove the geocentric light-time consistently. Preserve BJD_TDB as the reference. Publish the actual 1σ ephemeris prediction errors separately from the ±15-min operational window. State the observing-site coordinates, altitude and solar-altitude criteria used to call an event fully observable. The three archived v1/v2/v3 BJD_TDB CSV hashes match their JSON records; the issue is specifically the UTC/contacts interpretation, not those hashes.

### 7. The “pre-registered” fourth-event test was not publicly registered before the data were searched (pp. 2–3, 7–8; §§3.1, 6, 8)

The fourth transit is a valuable held-out test of the first three-event ephemeris, and its cadence was indeed already on disk but removed by QUALITY=0. The public chronology, however, is more nuanced than “pre-registered”: the first Zenodo deposit was created 2026-10-17 23:05 UTC with a three-event v1 record; the four-event v2 freeze was made 2026-10-17 22:20:50 UTC, before the first public deposit; and the fourth event was identified shortly after that freeze. Thus the event was not new data and the four-event timing solution was not frozen before inspecting it. The manuscript says this, but later calls the fourth transit “the first such test” of a publicly archived preregistration and broadly labels all predictions as pre-registered. The actual public prediction record can support the three-event holdout for the fourth event only if the v1 contents and freeze time were demonstrably immutable before its discovery; the cited first deposit metadata says its description already includes the later v2 four-transit file, so the public archive record alone does not establish that chronology. The separate GitHub history and first-deposit zip need explicit version provenance.

Clarify that this is a post hoc recovery of an already-observed, quality-flagged event that tests a previously frozen three-event ephemeris, not an out-of-sample new-observation confirmation. Separate v1, v2 and v3 prediction roles, and cite immutable version-specific records with timestamps and hashes. Do not use “pre-registered” without specifying which prediction and what was public at the time.

### 8. The neighbouring star's separation and flux accounting should use one consistent epoch/aperture model (pp. 4–5; §4.2; Figure 2)

Gaia DR3 confirms the neighbour's G=15.486, parallax 0.458 mas, proper motion (+1.69,-6.03) mas yr^-1 and GSP-Phot Teff≈5621 K, log g≈3.83, [M/H]≈-0.76; target values also match the TAP catalogue to quoted precision. The concurrent report identifies this same source as 9.5″ away at Gaia's reference epoch and models the difference images. The manuscript says “7–8″ away” at the TESS epoch, which may be plausible after proper motion but should be tabulated at each actual transit epoch; do not mix its 2016 Gaia separation with a 2023–2026 pixel offset. The flux ratio 10^(-0.4×3.64)=0.0350 and target aperture fraction 0.787 imply neighbour aperture fraction ≈0.0275 and required eclipse ≈36–40%, as stated. However, the 0.787 fraction is a TIC-based deblending estimate, not a direct constraint on the neighbour-hosted scenario; the other report's Gaia/PSF model and TRICERATOPS prior give different fractions. Show the aperture/PSF model and uncertainty that turns each Gaia source magnitude into the quoted aperture flux.

### 9. Search completeness and the flagged-cadence interpretation need stronger qualification (pp. 2, 8; §§2.2, 7)

The product description says QLP's original quality bit 13 (value 4196) marked “low precision points” using a field-wide criterion, and the MIT update notes that QLP flags bit 31 as poor-quality under internal metrics ([QLP update](https://tess.mit.edu/qlp-update/)). The paper's identification of bit 31 with the QLP flag is plausible and the local QLP FITS confirm the fourth event's 72/72 in-transit cadences carry value 2^30. But the manuscript calls the bit “a field-wide, time-based cut” and asserts that this is “exactly why a genuine transit can lie inside it.” A field-wide flag identifies cadence-level quality, not a cadence that is necessarily scientifically usable; the four-event confirmation and independent TARS/field controls help, but they do not make those flagged samples equivalent to normal data. Conversely, the fourth dip should not be discarded solely because of the bit.

The completeness claim that keeping flagged points drops known-planet recovery from 22/40 to 5/40 is potentially useful but lacks a described control sample, sector/cadence matching, recovery statistic, and uncertainty. The search spans Sectors 1–104 and 21,343 requested light-curve files, yet the sample pool is described only through Sector 104 footprint/TIC cuts. Report the actual reproducible sample query and provide the search code/data manifest, injection/recovery results, and the sector-edge mask details. The manuscript already rightly warns that SDE ranking is not the case for the individual event; retain that caution.

### 10. The companion framing and occurrence-rate prior overstate what radius can say (p. 6; §5)

Calling the object either a warm giant planet or brown dwarf is appropriately cautious, and “candidate, not planet” is the right title/abstract posture. The radius alone cannot classify mass. The numerical “prior is roughly even” is not supported by comparing a Kepler 8–11.3 R⊕ occurrence rate at 12.5–25 d around FGK stars to a brown-dwarf companion frequency for P<10 d from a different sample: the period domains, host populations, completeness and definitions differ, and no likelihood model maps these rates to this particular candidate. Remove the “roughly even” inference or provide comparable occurrence rates and an explicit prior calculation. The 8 R⊕ cutoff discussion cites Giacalone et al. (2021), which says its calibration sample and validation application was restricted to Rp<8 R⊕ because of the planet/brown-dwarf/low-mass-star degeneracy; it does not establish that no statistical-validation framework applies at all radii. Say that the authors choose not to apply TRICERATOPS as a planet validation here because its usual scenario set does not classify a target-hosted brown dwarf/low-mass star and the candidate remains physically ambiguous. The [Giacalone et al. paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2002.00691) is narrower than the manuscript's absolute claim.
