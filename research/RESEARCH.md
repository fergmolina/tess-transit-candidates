## (a) Executive summary: the ten findings that matter most

Ordered by how much each changes the team's current claims. Items 1-5 weaken or re-scope claims; 6-10 are confirmations and new levers.

1. **There is no statistical-validation path for TIC 89247735.** TRICERATOPS, the only maintained validation tool (VESPA was formally retired in 2023), was calibrated and applied "only [to] planets with best-fit planet radii Rp < 8 R_Earth" because giant planets, brown dwarfs and low-mass stars are "typically indistinguishable based on radius alone" (Giacalone et al. 2021). At 8.5 +/- 0.3 R_Earth the candidate is above that cut. TRICERATOPS's authors restricted calibration and validation to Rp < 8 R_Earth because the TP scenario lumps planet, brown dwarf and low-mass star together, and no TESS validation paper fetched here validates above that cut. No FPP will make it a validated planet; only radial velocities decide its nature. Any public text should say "transiting companion candidate (giant planet or brown dwarf)", not "planet candidate".
2. **The planet-vs-brown-dwarf prior at this radius and period is roughly 1:1, not planet-favoured.** Kepler occurrence for 8.0-11.3 R_Earth at 12.5-25 d around FGK stars is 0.06 (+0.07/-0.04) % (0.18 % for 11.3-16 R_Earth); close brown dwarfs occur at ~0.2 % for P < 10 d. The measured 0.76 RJ matches old 40-70 MJ brown dwarfs (model radii 0.81-0.85 RJ; TOI-569 b: 64 MJ, 0.75 RJ) as well as warm Saturns (NGTS-11 b: 0.34 MJ, 0.82 RJ). Irradiation is irrelevant at Teq ~ 560 K.
3. **Gaia astrometry says nothing at 18 d.** DR3 orbital solutions were searched only for P >= 10 d and RUWE/proper-motion-anomaly sensitivity peaks near 1000 d; the sub-100-microarcsec wobble is averaged out. The RUWE = 1.10 / non_single_star = 0 arguments in REPORT 8.4 and the external review should be dropped. The M-dwarf exclusion rests on the secondary-eclipse limit and on the Gaia RV error, and the latter, inverted through Katz et al.'s bright-star error formula (valid if grvs_mag <= 12, which the A4 query confirms; rv_nb_transits = 14 satisfies the >= 10 recommendation), bounds the companion at K <~ 2.2 km/s, i.e. M2 <~ 20-25 MJ (analyst estimate), which excludes M dwarfs and the upper half of the brown-dwarf range but not low-mass brown dwarfs.
4. **The S104 transit time of TIC 89247735 comes from a QLP file with a known, uncorrected timing error.** MAST: "A barycentric timing correction error was identified starting in Orbit 210 (second half of Sector 99) onward to Orbit 220 (end of Sector 104)", caused by an ephemeris file past its sampling range and then by a correction applied to spacecraft velocity instead of position; "MAST is in the process of updating these sector files with corrected timestamps" and Sectors 99, 100, 101, 102, 103 and 104 are all still marked as not yet redelivered (https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsp/qlp). The magnitude is not stated (the separate S74-79 error was up to +/- 3 min). The team's S104 file is v01, so the S104 time (BTJD 4196.806 +/- 1.8 min) that anchors both frozen ephemerides is affected by an error of unknown size; S66 and S93 (orbits before 210) are not. (SPOC DRN 142 also lists three reaction-wheel calibration episodes with large pointing dispersion in orbit 220 at TJD 4198.61-4198.72, 4200.81-4200.91 and 4203.00-4203.11; the S104 transit at 4196.81 precedes all three; merged from sub-search.) Re-time the S104 transit from a SPOC-calibrated product (TESS-SPOC S104 light curve or SPOC FFI cutouts) before anyone observes the 2027 transits. Separately, QLP switched to the TGLC methodology at S94, so the S104 depth comes from a different photometric method than S66/S93; the 2.5-sigma depth scatter should not be interpreted as astrophysical.
5. **An FPP for TIC 317134140 would not count as validation, and the literature precedent for its SNR regime is nine CHEOPS visits.** Giacalone et al. 2021: "FPP alone does not appear to be a reliable predictor of TOI disposition when SNR < 15"; the signal is at SNR 9.9. HD 93963 A b (141.5 ppm, TESS SNR 6.7) was "not listed as a TOI owing to the low significance of its signal" and was confirmed only after nine CHEOPS transits; TOI-561 b (290 ppm, SNR 10 in one sector) needed RVs. The pre-registered S108-S110 test is the right design; TRICERATOPS is a supporting number at best.
6. **The CTOI route is closed until there is a refereed paper.** Since 2026-08-19 ExoFOP requires candidates to be "published in a peer-reviewed journal that offers online access before being uploaded"; an RNAAS qualifies only if the detection and vetting methods were themselves previously refereed. Every confirmed amateur-found TESS planet (TOI 813 b, the Visual Survey Group's discoveries) reached the record through a professional co-author and a refereed paper. Rabtsevich likewise has no CTOI; he obtained TESS DDT program 100 (2-min cadence, TIC 4206066, active; on the S110 target list) and a Zenodo preprint with TRICERATOPS FPP 0.03-0.04 and no validation claim. The team's Zenodo/GitHub deposit is pre-registration, not publication.
7. **The team's single-transit methodology is at or above the published standard.** Hawthorn et al. 2024 (1.42 million SPOC-FFI stars, 85 duotransit candidates, 60 new) discard every QUALITY > 0 cadence and reject 88 % of visually plausible pairs as asteroids (23 %), EBs (52 %), blended EBs (8 %) and systematics (5 %); they have no cross-star coincidence control. The team keeps the ramp cadences, refits a local baseline, and runs per-CCD field and coincidence controls. Yields are consistent: the Hawthorn and TIaRA rates predict of order 0.2-0.6 duotransit candidates in Pool B and about 10 before completeness losses in the all-sky run (analyst estimates).
8. **Inverted-light-curve nulls are known to under-count asymmetric systematics; the fix is a scrambled null.** Kepler DR25 states that inversion requires false alarms to be "perfectly symmetric (in shape and frequency) under flux inversion, which is not true" and found only ~60 % as many false alarms in inverted data; the remedy was year-scrambled light curves alongside inversion, and reporting completeness (injection) and reliability (inversion + scrambling) as separate quantities. The team's section 7.3 finding is this effect; the "~4 false positives at SDE 14.3" is a lower bound on reliability, and the only true completeness numbers are the single-transit injection results.
9. **TRICERATOPS is runnable in a separate venv and should be run on both objects for the neighbour-blend number, not for an FPP headline.** PyPI 1.1.0 pins numpy < 2 and scipy < 1.14 on Python 3.12 (numpy >= 2.1 on 3.13) and pytransit == 2.2; the project venv (numpy 2.5) cannot host it. For 317134140 all inputs exist (SPOC apertures, SOAR speckle curve on ExoFOP); for 89247735 the aperture must be supplied by hand and the G = 15.49 neighbour at 8-9.5 arcsec will dominate the NFPP, which is exactly the number worth having.
10. **Two cheap, independent on-target tests remain unexploited.** (a) The TGLC method yields PSF-deblended light curves for every Gaia star to T = 16; the MAST HLSP stops at S55, but the open-source `tglc` package runs on any sector, so S66/S93/S104 light curves for both TIC 89247735 and its G = 15.49 neighbour can be generated locally: the transit should be in the target's curve and absent from the neighbour's. (b) Gaia DR3 `rv_amplitude_robust`, `rv_chisq_pvalue` and `rv_renormalised_gof` are published for G_RVS <= 12 and are the designed excess-scatter tests; one archive query. Also confirmed: TIC 317134140 is a 2-min target in S108, S109 and S110 on three different camera/CCD combinations, so the decisive data will exist at the cadence the predictions assume.

**How the field handles systematics, flags and aliases.**
* Quality flags: Hawthorn et al. discard every cadence with QUALITY > 0 and segment light curves at gaps > 2.4 h (Section 3.1). They do not model scattered light; 39 of 736 visually accepted events were later attributed to spacecraft systematics. This is weaker than what the team now does in the single-transit pass (keeping QLP bit 2^30 cadences, raw SAP flux, local baseline refit, per-CCD field control and a cross-star coincidence control, REPORT section 10). The team's periodic search, which keeps QUALITY == 0, is at the Hawthorn standard and shares its blind spot for orbit-start ramps.
* Field-wide systematics: no published duotransit search uses a per-sector cross-star coincidence test as the team does; Hawthorn relies on visual inspection and DV-report review. The team's coincidence control is a genuine methodological advance and should be described as such when publishing.
* Period determination from two transits: the standard is P_n = dT/n with n_max set by the covered baseline, then a likelihood for each alias from the transit duration, impact parameter and stellar density (MonoTools; Hawthorn Section 3.3; Cooke et al. 2021). The team's `single_rank.py` does the coverage-based exclusion but does not weight the surviving aliases by the duration/density likelihood. For TIC 89247735 this is moot (two consecutive S93 transits fix the period), but for the all-sky run the duration-density alias likelihood is the missing standard step.
* False positives in the single-transit regime: Hawthorn's numbers imply 88 % of visually plausible duotransit pairs are false positives (asteroids, EBs, blends, systematics). The team's inverted-null comparison (158 real vs 160 inverted matched stars) says the same thing from the other side. Both agree that two matched events at FFI precision do not make a candidate; Hawthorn still publishes two-event candidates because each is individually inspected and submitted to TFOP for follow-up, not because two events are statistically sufficient.

**Yields compared with REPORT section 10.** The following are analyst estimates, not literature values; they assume the published per-star rates transfer from SPOC-FFI samples to the team's bright (Tmag <= 11.5) QLP pools with 2-9 sectors each, and they ignore the team's 38-59 % end-to-end completeness. Using the Hawthorn rate (85 candidates per 1.42 million FFI stars, about 6 per 100,000), Pool B (3,397 stars) should contain about 0.2 duotransit candidates; using the TIaRA K-dwarf detection rate (3.4e-3 planets per star over Year 1 + 3, of which about 10 % monotransits and 5 % duotransits), Pool B should contain about 11 detectable planets in total, about 1 monotransit and about 0.6 duotransits, with most of the periodic ones already catalogued because the pool is bright. One surviving multi-event candidate is therefore the expected order of magnitude, not a deficit. For the 57,853-star all-sky run the same rates predict about 200 detectable planets of which roughly 20 monotransits and 10 duotransits before completeness losses; the periodic ones are mostly known TOIs, so the new-candidate yield should be dominated by long-period single and double events, exactly the regime where the team's end-to-end completeness is 38-59 % (REPORT section 10). After that completeness, expect of order 5-10 genuine new long-period candidates from the all-sky run, dominated by P > 27 d objects.

### Q2. Statistical validation without radial velocities

**Current standard.**
* VESPA is retired. Morton, Giacalone & Bryson 2023, RNAAS 7, 5, "A Recommendation to Retire VESPA for Exoplanet Validation": "VESPA is no longer maintained, and does not implement the best available analysis"; they recommend "retiring it in favor of the newer, actively maintained TRICERATOPS package", which also accounts for Gaia field stars. https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/2515-5172/acd9a6
* TRICERATOPS (Giacalone et al. 2021, AJ 161, 24). Validation thresholds: FPP < 0.015 and NFPP < 1e-3; "likely planet" FPP < 0.5 and NFPP < 1e-3; "likely nearby false positive" NFPP > 0.1. https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.00691 . Later papers tightened this: Giacalone et al. 2022 use FPP < 0.01 and NFPP < 1e-3 with 20 repeated runs and report mean and scatter (https://arxiv.org/html/2201.12661v2); the hot-Neptune validation paper of Castro-Gonzalez et al. 2022 uses FPP < 3e-3 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.08490, snippet only). TRICERATOPS+ (2025) adds multi-colour photometry (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.02782, snippet only).
* Two statements in Giacalone et al. 2021 directly limit what the tool can say about the team's candidates (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.00691):
  1. Radius cut: "we only include planets with best-fit planet radii Rp < 8 R_Earth under the TP scenario", because giant planets, brown dwarfs and low-mass stars are "typically indistinguishable based on radius alone". TIC 89247735 at 8.5 +/- 0.3 R_Earth is above this cut. TRICERATOPS's authors excluded objects of this size from calibration and validation, and no TESS validation paper fetched here validates above that cut, because the "TP" probability lumps planet, brown dwarf and late-M dwarf together. This is a weakening finding for any plan to "validate" TIC 89247735 without RVs.
  2. SNR: "FPP alone does not appear to be a reliable predictor of TOI disposition when SNR < 15". TIC 317134140 has SNR 9.9 (REPORT section 6.3). An FPP for it would be supporting evidence, not validation.
* Other tools in use: DAVE (Kostov et al. 2019) for flux- and pixel-level vetting; LEO-Vetter (Kunimoto et al. 2025, accepted AJ), a fully automated Robovetter-style flux- and pixel-level vetter with 91 % completeness and 97 % reliability against noise/systematic false alarms on simulated data, used to reduce ~20,000 detections in ~200,000 M-dwarf light curves to 172 candidates; released on PyPI as `leo-vetter` (release 2026-04-29). https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10619 ; https://pypi.org/user/mkuni/ (snippet only). Rabtsevich's TIC 4206066 protocol used LEO-Vetter plus TRICERATOPS (DECISIONS item 2), so adopting both would put the team at parity with the reference work.

**TRICERATOPS inputs (from Giacalone et al. 2021 and from the 1.1.0 source inspected in the wheel):** TIC ID; list of sectors; the photometric aperture (pixel coordinates) used in each sector, which `get_spoc_apertures()` fills automatically only for SPOC 2-min targets and which must be supplied by hand for QLP/FFI targets; a phase-folded light curve (time, normalised flux, flux error) around the primary transit; the orbital period (fixed, circular) and the user-entered depth; stellar M, R, Teff (defaults to TIC values, with `estimate_stellar_params()` for gaps); optional contrast curve(s) with filter, otherwise unresolved companions are assumed excluded beyond 2.2 arcsec. Scenarios: TP, EB, EBx2P on the target; PTP, PEB, PEBx2P, STP, SEB, SEBx2P for an unresolved bound companion; DTP, DEB, DEBx2P, BTP, BEB, BEBx2P for an unresolved foreground/background star; NTP, NEB, NEBx2P for each resolved nearby star within 10 pixels whose required depth is below 1. The period prior is a broken power law with a break at 10 d; the radius prior spans 0.5-20 R_Earth; occurrence-rate priors are deliberately omitted.

**Can it be run on the team's two candidates?**
* TIC 317134140 (0.5719 d, 185 ppm, SPOC 2-min in S6/S87/S98/S107): yes, all inputs exist. SPOC apertures are retrieved automatically; the phase-folded, TOI-2514.01-masked light curve exists in `results/vet/317134140/`; the SOAR/HRCam speckle contrast curve (I band, dmag 4.6 at 1 arcsec, 2021-10-01) and Shane/ShARCS J/Ks curves are on ExoFOP (REPORT section 6.8) and can be passed as `contrast_curve_file`. The EBx2P scenario addresses the 1.1437-d equal-eclipse binary the team flagged. Expect a useful NFPP (nearest Gaia neighbours are 24 and 32 arcsec away and need 0.5-1.4 % eclipses) but an FPP that the literature would not accept as validation at SNR ~10.
* TIC 89247735 (18.32 d, 1.0 %, QLP only): it can be run, with a hand-built aperture per sector and a phase-folded light curve from the four raw-SAP transits, and it would quantify the NEB probability for the G = 15.49 neighbour at 8-9.5 arcsec (which lies inside the 10-pixel search radius and needs a 23 % eclipse, so its NEB scenario will carry weight unless a contrast curve or ground-based photometry excludes it). But the result cannot be reported as a validation: the candidate is above the 8 R_Earth cut, and the TP probability does not separate planet from brown dwarf. It is still worth running to put a number on the neighbour-blend branch (the only unresolved pixel-level issue, REPORT section 8.3).

**Installability (checked 2026-10-17, no install performed).** `triceratops` 1.1.0 is on PyPI (versions 0.1 to 1.1.0); the wheel `triceratops-1.1.0-py3-none-any.whl` downloaded cleanly with `pip download --no-deps` into the scratch directory. Its metadata requires Python >= 3.8 and pins `pytransit == 2.2`, plus, for Python < 3.13, `numpy < 2` and `scipy < 1.14`; for Python >= 3.13 it allows `numpy >= 2.1` and `scipy >= 1.14`. The project venv (Python 3.12, numpy 2.5.4, scipy 1.18.1; DECISIONS item 1) therefore cannot host it without downgrading numpy; use a separate venv (Python 3.12 with numpy 1.26, or Python 3.13 with numpy >= 2.1; the latter avoids the downgrade but pytransit 2.2 under numpy 2 is untested here). Other pins: pandas >= 0.23.4, astropy >= 4.0, astroquery >= 0.4.6, lightkurve >= 2.0, emcee >= 3.0.2, numba >= 0.52, celerite >= 0.4, mechanicalsoup, seaborn, arviz, corner, pyrr, setuptools < 81 on Python >= 3.12. Installation in a conda Python 3.8 environment is what the README recommends (https://github.com/stevengiacalone/triceratops). The dry run checked wheel metadata only; `pytransit == 2.2` and `celerite` (a C++ extension) are old and their build or wheel availability on Python 3.13 / macOS arm64 was not verified.

### Q3. Brown dwarf vs giant planet vs late-M dwarf at 0.76 R_Jup, P = 18.3 d, K-dwarf host

**Radius overlap is total.** The transiting brown-dwarf population now exceeds 50 confirmed objects (Vowell et al. 2025, AJ 170, 68; 11 new objects of 25-128 MJ; https://www.ipac.caltech.edu/publication/2025AJ....170...68V). Mukherjee et al. 2025 fit 46 transiting BDs with masses 12.9-89 MJ and measured radii from 0.75 RJ (TOI-569 b) to 3.1 RJ, most at 0.8-1.4 RJ; age-averaged non-irradiated model radii are 0.81-0.85 RJ for 40-70 MJ at 4-10 Gyr (KOI-205 b 0.853 RJ at 39.7 MJ, TOI-852 b 0.837 RJ at 53.7 MJ, TOI-2543 b 0.809 RJ at 67.6 MJ); irradiation inflates radii only at log F >= 8.5-9, i.e. Teq >= 1450 K (https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08249v1). At Teq ~ 560 K the candidate is in the non-irradiated regime, so a 0.76 RJ companion is exactly what an old 50-70 MJ brown dwarf should look like (TOI-569 b: 64.1 MJ, 0.75 RJ, 6.56 d; TOI-1406 b: 46.0 MJ, 0.86 RJ, 10.57 d; both > 3 Gyr; https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.01943), and also what a warm Saturn looks like (NGTS-11 b: 0.344 MJ, 0.817 RJ, 35.46 d, Teq 435 K; https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00006). The REPORT's statement that photometry cannot distinguish the three is fully supported; if anything the radius slightly favours the brown-dwarf branch over an inflated giant, because 0.76 RJ is at the compact end of both populations.

**Occurrence priors.** Giant planets 8-16 R_Earth around FGK stars: 1.6 (+0.3/-0.3) % for P < 50 d and 2.6 (+0.5/-0.4) % for P < 100 d (Kunimoto & Matthews 2020; hot Jupiters 0.43 % at P < 6.25 d; https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.05296). Brown dwarfs at P < 10 d: about 0.2 %, "about six times smaller than the frequency of hot Jupiters in the same period range" (Csizmadia et al. 2015; https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.05763); RV surveys give 0.6-0.8 % for BD companions overall (Ma & Ge 2014, snippet only). A 2026 Gaia DR3 astrometric study (Wallace & Casey) argues 10.4 (+0.8/-0.6) % of Sun-like stars within 200 pc have BD-mass companions at P < 5 yr and that the desert may not exist, while cautioning they "are unable to make a definitive statement without epoch data" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03539); treat as unsettled. In the specific cell that matters, Kunimoto & Matthews' Table 8 gives for FGK stars at P = 12.5-25 d: 0.06 (+0.07/-0.04) % for 8.0-11.3 R_Earth and 0.18 (+0.10/-0.09) % for 11.3-16 R_Earth (K stars alone: < 0.63 % upper limit and 0.45 (+0.41/-0.27) %). Net: for an 8.5 R_Earth object at 18 d the giant-planet occurrence (about 0.1-0.25 % per star) and the brown-dwarf occurrence (about 0.2 % at P < 10 d, and Csizmadia et al. suspect BD frequency declines faster with decreasing period than giant planets, so it may be higher at 18 d) are the same order of magnitude. The prior is roughly 1:1 between planet and brown dwarf, not planet-favoured; the REPORT's three-way agnosticism is correct and should be kept, and any public text should avoid "planet candidate" as the default label without the qualifier "or brown dwarf".

**Photometric and archival discriminants, in order of power.**
1. Secondary eclipse: the team's 2-sigma limit of 700 ppm bounds a thermally emitting companion to Teff < 2680 K in the TESS band (REPORT section 8.4); this excludes M dwarfs above the latest types but no brown dwarf at Teq 560 K. Standard and sufficient for what it can do.
2. Ellipsoidal and beaming modulation (Shporer 2017 review, https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1703.00496): my own estimate for a 50 MJ companion at a/R = 31 around a 0.7 M_Sun star gives an ellipsoidal amplitude of a few ppm and a Doppler-beaming amplitude of about 65 ppm (K ~ 4.9 km/s); for 13 MJ the beaming amplitude is ~17 ppm. Neither is recoverable in QLP photometry at an 18-d period, where the scattered-light systematics the team documented (REPORT section 5.2) dominate. Not a useful test here.
3. Gaia astrometry: Gaia DR3 astrometric orbital solutions were searched only for "from 10 days to the duration covered by the observations of the star divided by 0.6" and the processing targets "binary stars with periods of at least several days" (Halbwachs et al. 2023, Section 5.1 and 3.1; https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.05726); RUWE is most sensitive at P ~ 1000 d and to nearby massive faint companions (snippet only, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.12146); the proper-motion-anomaly method is "drastically" less sensitive for periods shorter than the 1038-d EDR3 window because of time-window smearing (Kervella et al. 2022, Section 2.3; https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.10912). An 18-d, 50 MJ companion at 104 pc produces a photocentre wobble of order 50-80 microarcsec, and even a 0.1 M_Sun companion gives only ~100 microarcsec, both averaged down by Gaia's sampling (analyst estimate from a = 0.113 AU and the mass ratio). Consequence: RUWE = 1.10 and non_single_star = 0 constrain no companion mass at 18 d, up to and including stellar. The REPORT (section 8.4) and the external review cite them against a stellar companion; the M-dwarf exclusion actually rests entirely on the secondary-eclipse limit and on the Gaia RV error, and should be stated that way.
4. Gaia DR3 epoch-RV statistics: for sources with grvs_mag <= 12 the archive publishes `rv_amplitude_robust` (max-min of the per-transit RVs after outlier removal), `rv_chisq_pvalue` (constancy p-value, needs rv_nb_transits >= 3) and `rv_renormalised_gof` (https://gea.esac.esa.int/archive/documentation/GDR3/Data_processing/chap_cu6spe/sec_cu6spe_intro/ssec_cu6spe_dataprod.html). For binaries `rv_amplitude_robust` approximates 2K within ~10 % in most cases (snippet only, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.11293). TIC 89247735 has G = 11.85 and 14 RV transits; a K4-5 dwarf has G_RVS roughly 0.6 mag brighter than G, so these fields are probably populated. Caution on scale: the star's 0.54 km/s is the *combined* `radial_velocity_error`, and Katz et al. 2023 quote a median combined precision of 1.3 km/s at G_RVS = 12 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05902); the per-epoch precision at this magnitude is several times larger, so `rv_amplitude_robust` for a constant star with 14 epochs is already several km/s wide. A 13 MJ companion (K ~ 1.3 km/s, peak-to-peak 2.6 km/s) is therefore invisible in these fields; a 50 MJ companion (peak-to-peak ~10 km/s) is a check, not a guaranteed detection; `rv_chisq_pvalue` and `rv_renormalised_gof` are the designed excess-scatter tests. The external reviewer's argument (0.54 km/s over 14 transits excludes an M dwarf) does hold, and it reaches further than stated: Katz et al. 2023, Section 3.6.2, Eq. 1 defines the bright-star error as eps = sqrt((sqrt(pi/2N) sigma)^2 + 0.113^2) with sigma the standard deviation of the epoch RVs and 0.113 km/s a calibration floor (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.05902), so intrinsic variability inflates it. Inverting for N = 14 and eps = 0.54 km/s gives an epoch scatter sigma ~ 1.6 km/s, which caps any orbital semi-amplitude at K <~ 2.2 km/s even if all the scatter were orbital (analyst estimate, assumes sigma = K/sqrt 2 for well-sampled phases). At P = 18.3 d around a 0.7 M_Sun star that is M2 <~ 20-25 MJ, i.e. the Gaia RV error excludes M dwarfs and the upper half of the brown-dwarf mass range, leaving planets and low-mass brown dwarfs (<~ 20 MJ) open. Katz et al. recommend using the variability indices only with >= 10 transits and flag variability at rv_chisq_pvalue <= 0.01 with rv_renormalised_gof > 4; one archive query settles what the fields say.
5. Radial velocities remain decisive: K ~ 30 m/s (0.3 MJ), 100 m/s (1 MJ), 1.3 km/s (13 MJ), 8 km/s (0.1 M_Sun) (REPORT section 8.4 and 9).

**What TESS brown dwarfs looked like before RVs.** Carmichael et al. 2020's TOI-569 b and TOI-1406 b were TOIs with ~0.4-0.7 % transits and TESS-only radii of 0.75-0.86 RJ around slightly evolved F/G stars (https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.01943); Zhang et al. 2026's TOI-4776 b (32 MJ, 1.018 RJ, 10.41 d) and TOI-5422 b (27.7 MJ, 0.815 RJ, 5.38 d) likewise entered the TOI list as giant-planet candidates (https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05115). All were sorted out by TFOP SG2/SG4 spectroscopy, which is the only route that has ever worked for this class.

**RV access for an independent researcher.**
* TFOP membership is open: applicants need the "desire, ability, and access to appropriate facilities" (observer members) or can join as general members without a facility; citizen scientists are explicitly invited and should supply 2-3 examples of prior results; no sponsor is required; applicants agree to the TFOP WG Charter and Publication Policy (both updated 2026-07-12). https://tess.mit.edu/followup/apply-join-tfop/ ; https://tess.mit.edu/followup/ . SG2 (recon spectroscopy, chair Sam Quinn) and SG4 (precise RV, chair Dave Latham) are the groups that would observe a TOI promoted from a CTOI; the normal path is CTOI submission -> TOI promotion -> TFOP queue.
* Instruments: CHIRON on the SMARTS 1.5 m reaches 5-20 m/s for K dwarfs with V = 7-12 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3971); V ~ 12.5 is at its faint edge but a 1.3 km/s brown-dwarf signal needs only ~100 m/s precision. LCO NRES was designed for < 3 m/s at V < 12 (design projection, snippet only). ESO and NOIRLab time is proposal-based and in practice requires an institutional collaborator.
* Amateur RV: commercially available fibre-fed echelles (Shelyak eShel) on 0.5-m telescopes reach ~100 m/s, ~50 m/s in the best cases, on bright stars (Csak et al. 2014, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3532); the 2006 Spectrashift tau Boo detection (K = 471 m/s, V = 4.5) used a 0.4 m telescope (https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0609468). At V ~ 12.5 no amateur system reaches the SNR for even the 8 km/s M-dwarf case in reasonable exposure times. Realistic conclusion: the brown-dwarf/planet question will be settled by a professional 1-2 m spectrograph via TFOP, not by amateur RV; the independent researcher's lever is the CTOI submission and a clean pre-registration package.

### Q4. Ultra-short-period small planets like the TIC 317134140 signal

**Occurrence.** Sanchis-Ojeda et al. 2014 (ApJ 787, 47): USP planets (P < 1 d) occur around 0.15 +/- 0.05 % of F dwarfs, 0.51 +/- 0.07 % of G, 0.83 +/- 0.18 % of K, 1.10 +/- 0.40 % of M; "essentially all USP planets are in systems with other SP planets" with periods 1-50 d; their candidates have depths 37-978 ppm and they "eliminated every candidate with a transit signal-to-noise ratio less than 12" (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.2379). Winn, Sanchis-Ojeda & Rappaport 2018: USPs are "about as common as hot Jupiters", "almost always smaller than 2" R_Earth, and often in compact multis (https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03303). A 0.57-d, 1.4 R_Earth planet interior to the 6.4-d TOI-2514.01 is therefore a high-prior architecture: the companion statistic raises the prior for a USP in a known small-planet system by more than an order of magnitude relative to a random star. The REPORT (section 6.5) says this qualitatively; it can be made quantitative.

**TESS USPs found at this depth.**
* TOI-561 b (Weiss et al. 2021, AJ 161, 56): P = 0.45 d, 1.42-1.45 R_Earth, SPOC depth 290 ppm, detected in one 2-min sector (S8) from 55 transits at SNR 10.0 on a Tmag 9.49 star (R = 0.832 R_Sun); passed odd/even and ghost tests, the centroid test was the one not passed cleanly (source within 11 arcsec); confirmed with HIRES and HARPS-N RVs and ground-based NEB clearing (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2009.03071).
* HD 93963 A b (TESS + CHEOPS, 2022): P = 1.04 d, 1.35 R_Earth, 141.5 +/- 8.5 ppm, TESS SNR ~6.7, "not listed as a TOI owing to the low significance of its signal"; confirmed by nine CHEOPS transit visits on a V = 9.2 star (https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13920).
* TOI-500 b (0.548 d, 1.16 R_Earth, 246 +/- 27 ppm, Ks 7.73) and TOI-2260 b (0.352 d, 1.62 R_Earth, 313 +/- 35 ppm) were statistically validated by Giacalone et al. 2022 with TRICERATOPS at FPP < 0.01, NFPP < 1e-3, using TFOP ground photometry to clear nearby stars and high-resolution imaging to shrink the companion parameter space; no secondary eclipse at >= 3 sigma in any case (https://arxiv.org/html/2201.12661v2).
The team's signal (185 ppm, SNR 9.9 over four 2-min sectors on a Tmag 10.67 star) is shallower and on a fainter star than TOI-561 b and in the same SNR regime as HD 93963 A b, which the field did not accept until CHEOPS saw it nine times. The precedent is clear: a sub-10-sigma USP is confirmed by independent photometry (CHEOPS, or more TESS sectors) or by RVs, not by stacking the discovery data differently.

**False-positive modes for sub-day signals and the standard tests (Sanchis-Ojeda et al. 2014).** Odd/even depth differences at > 3 sigma removed ~40 % of their initial candidates (equal-depth EBs at 2P are the main astrophysical contaminant); the centroid test removed half of the remainder and leaves a residual background-binary probability "of order 5 %"; ellipsoidal variations (none above 3 sigma allowed), secondary eclipses (seven detected at planet-like amplitudes), ingress duration (five candidates with ingress > 45 min rejected), and pulsator rejection (> 10 significant frequencies at 1-10 c/d). Diluted contact binaries are a specific risk at these periods: the W UMa period distribution has a sharp cut-off at 0.22 d, a maximum near 0.27-0.40 d and a tail to ~1-1.5 d (Rucinski 2007, https://arxiv.org/pdf/0708.3020; Stepien 2006, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0701529), so a background contact binary at 1.1437 d is possible but would be quasi-sinusoidal rather than box-shaped; the team's box-vs-sinusoid test (delta chi-square 104 vs 31) is the right discriminator and should be kept in the record. Instrumental periods: momentum dumps occur every 2-6 d (3.125 d in S6 and S9), one rotation study lists dump intervals of 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 5 and 13.7 d and reports a "previously unreported periodic systematic around 0.73 days" as a possible overtone of the 1.5-d dump cadence (snippet only, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10847). A 0.5719-d signal is not a known spacecraft period, but the sector-specific dump intervals of S6, S87, S98 and S107 should be checked against 0.5719 d and its 2x/3x/4x multiples; the team's shared-period test across 1,637 stars (REPORT section 6.8) already argues against a common instrumental origin.

**How 3-5 sigma per sector becomes a planet.** The TESS norm is to let SPOC/QLP multi-sector stacking reach MES >= 7.1, promote to TOI, then (i) TFOP SG1 photometry to clear nearby stars, (ii) SG3 imaging for a contrast curve, (iii) TRICERATOPS FPP < 0.01 for small planets, and (iv) independent photometry (CHEOPS) or RV for anything the vetting leaves ambiguous. The team's pre-registered S108-S110 test is the TESS-only equivalent of step (iv) and is the right design; the CHEOPS archival visit (REPORT section 6.9) is the cheaper version of the HD 93963 route and should be pulled now.


### Q5. QLP and TESS FFI data quality

**What bit 2^30 (value 1073741824) is, as far as the public record goes.** The QLP data-product description (Huang, Nov 2020, supplement to the RNAAS articles; https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsps/qlp/hlsp_qlp_tess_ffi_all_tess_v1_data-prod-desc.pdf) says QLP adopts the SPOC FFI bit definitions and adds one bit of its own: bits 1 (attitude tweak), 3 (4, coarse pointing), 4 (8, Earth pointing), 5 (16, Argabrightening), 6 (32, reaction-wheel desaturation), 8 (128, manual exclude), 11 (1024, cosmic ray on collateral pixels), 12 (2048, "Straylight from Earth or Moon in camera FOV"), and QLP bit 13 (value printed as 4196, actually 4096 in the files) = "Low precision points. The cadence was excluded because low precision data occurred in a large fraction of the stars". Kunimoto et al. 2021 (RNAAS 5, 234; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.05542, snippet only) repeat that QLP marks poor-quality cadences with 4096 and ORs in the SPOC bits. Bit 2^30 appears in none of: that document, the HLSP README (https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsps/qlp/hlsp_qlp_tess_ffi_all_tess_v1_readme.txt), the HLSP landing page (https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsp/qlp), or Kunimoto et al. 2022 (https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2211.04386). The definition lives only on MIT's own QLP page (https://tess.mit.edu/qlp/): a 2023-11-21 note says the QLP poor-quality flag "previously used the unused-by-FFI bit 13 (2^12)" and was moved to bit 31 "to avoid future clashes with POC changes"; a 2025-03-31 note says that from Sector 77 the spacecraft flags are taken from TICA keywords, "bits 3, 6, and 12 are set to match the SPOC FFI header values", and "We also set bit 31 for cadences flagged as poor quality by internal QLP metrics"; the flag table lists bit 31 (value 2^30) simply as "QLP quality flag". The "internal QLP metrics" are not defined anywhere fetched, and lightkurve's TessQualityFlags defines no bit above 32768, so its default bitmasks never remove 2^30 (merged from sub-search). Local inspection of the team's own files for TIC 89247735 (QLP v01, read with astropy) shows: S13 has columns KSPSAP_FLUX / KSPSAP_FLUX_ERR and QUALITY values only from {0, 2048, 4096, 4100, 4132, 6144, ...}, i.e. no bit 2^30; S66, S93 and S104 have columns DET_FLUX / DET_FLUX_ERR / SYS_RM_FLUX and carry bit 2^30 on 19.1 %, 17.7 % and 24.9 % of cadences respectively, always ORed with 0, 32, 128 or 2048. The external review's empirical reading (orbit-start scattered-light ramp) is consistent with this, but the criterion is not published; the team should say "QLP internal poor-quality flag (bit 31), empirically concentrated in the orbit-start ramp" rather than "scattered-light flag", which is bit 12 (2048, predicted stray light).

**Two QLP facts that bear directly on TIC 89247735.**
1. "As of Sector 94, QLP is now using the TESS-Gaia Light Curve (TGLC) methodology to produce light curves" (https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsp/qlp). The S104 light curve is therefore a PSF-forward-model product while S66 and S93 are aperture products. This explains the NaN error columns from S94 on (DECISIONS item 14) and is a plausible cause of the 2.5-sigma scatter among the three QLP depths (10,003 / 10,835 / 9,834 ppm, REPORT section 8.3), which the team attributed to "sector-dependent crowding correction". A cross-pipeline depth comparison should not be used as evidence either way.
2. "A barycentric timing correction error was identified starting in Orbit 210 (second half of Sector 99) onward to Orbit 220 (end of Sector 104)"; it arose "in part due to changing interpolation library backends ... silencing an ephemeris file that had reached the end of its sampling range", and from Orbit 215 "barycentric timing correction was performed on spacecraft velocity values instead of position"; "MAST is in the process of updating these sector files with corrected timestamps", and Sectors 99-104 are all still asterisked as not redelivered; the download links are v01 (same page). The magnitude is not given; the S74-79 analogue was "up to roughly +/- 3.0 minutes" and was fixed in v02 files. The team's S104 file is v01. The S104 transit time (BTJD 4196.80603 +/- 1.8 min) anchors the v1 and v2 ephemerides (REPORT section 8.5) and the quoted +/- 15 min prediction window, so the ephemeris carries a timing error of unknown size, possibly comparable to its formal uncertainty. Because the error is in QLP's own barycentric correction (TICA-calibrated inputs are not the independent reference), the check must use a SPOC-calibrated product: the TESS-SPOC S104 light curve, or the SPOC FFI time stamps in the team's own TESScut cutouts (the default TESScut product), and the S104 transit should be re-timed from those before any observer is pointed at the 2027 transits.

**Pipelines and scattered light.** TESS-SPOC (Caldwell et al. 2020) applies the SPOC PDC cotrending; QLP uses global (nebuliser, later fitsh) plus local background subtraction and a Kepler-spline detrend with 0.75-1.5 d break spacing (data-product description, KSPBSMIN/KSPBSMAX; Kunimoto 2022 notes the global step was disabled in S40-41 after it caused discontinuities in S39); TGLC forward-models the FFI with an effective PSF using Gaia DR3 positions and magnitudes as priors and reaches "<~2 % at 16th TESS magnitude even in crowded fields", publishing both aperture and PSF light curves to T = 16 (Han & Brandt 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.03704); DIAmante uses difference imaging and a random forest (Montalto 2020); the T16 project (2026) released 83.7 million "uniformly detrended and systematics-corrected" Cycle-1 light curves to T = 16 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18579). No fetched 2022-2026 paper ranks these pipelines for orbit-start ramps specifically; eleanor and GSFC-ELEANOR-LITE were not fetched in this survey. Two practical conclusions: (i) for the periodic search on bright stars the team's tested choice (QLP/TESS-SPOC, QUALITY == 0, edge trim) is standard, and the team's own experiment showing that keeping bit-2^30 cadences in the periodic search destroys known-signal recovery (DECISIONS item 30) is the right basis for keeping the two-pass design; (ii) for the unresolved neighbour question, TGLC is the right method, because it produces a PSF-deblended light curve for every Gaia star to T = 16, including the G = 15.49 neighbour of TIC 89247735 itself. The MAST TGLC HLSP covers only Sectors 1-55 (last updated 2025-12-07) and MAST "recommends that users run the tglc package" for newer sectors, which "can be used to produce similar quality light curves for any sector and any star" (https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsp/tglc). Running `tglc` locally on S66/S93/S104 for both stars is the cheapest remaining on-target test and uses a method independent of the team's centroid estimator. The T16 benchmark (Hartman et al. 2025, PASP 137, 024501) found "no single project is best everywhere", with T16 beating QLP in 78 % and SPOC PDCSAP in 70 % of light curves, and TESSExtractor (2026) notes TGLC "produces noticeably deeper primary and secondary eclipses compared to the aperture-based pipelines" because it deblends (merged from sub-search), so TGLC depths for the target should be compared with the QLP S104 depth, which is already TGLC-method.

### Q7. Null calibration practice

**The Kepler standard separates two quantities.** Thompson et al. 2018 (DR25): "The completeness of the vetting process is measured by vetting thousands of injected transits" and "Catalog reliability is measured by vetting signals found in scrambled and inverted Kepler light curves" (Section I.1; https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1710.06758). Inversion multiplies the zero-mean flux by -1 so that "quasi-sinusoidal signals due to instrumental noise, contact and near-contact binaries, and stellar variability can still create detections"; scrambling moves the data "in coherent chunks of one year" to "keep the coherency of the instrumental and stellar noise". The explicit pitfall: "the false alarms would need to be perfectly symmetric (in shape and frequency) under flux inversion, which is not true. For example, stellar oscillations and star-spots are not sine waves and SPSDs will not appear the same under inversion", the rolling band being the exception because it "is mostly symmetric"; the inverted set produced "only ~60 % as many" false alarms as the observed set. That is precisely the under-count the team found for Pool B (REPORT section 7.3: inverted null 0/300 above SDE 12 while real FFI systematics cluster at 13.5 and 19 d), and the Kepler remedy was to use scrambling alongside inversion and to quote reliability as a function of MES and period from both. Hippke & Heller 2019 give SDE ~7 for a 1 % false-positive rate for both BLS and TLS, but on synthetic 110-ppm white-noise light curves (https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.02015); the team's empirical filtered thresholds (99th percentile 11.0 in Pool A, 10.6 in Pool B) show why that number cannot be used on real multi-year data. Bryson et al. 2020 give the working formula, R_FA = 1 - (N_FA/N_notFA) (1 - E_FA)/E_FA with E_FA the fraction of inverted/scrambled detections the vetter rejects, and warn that "The inverted and scrambled data are designed to measure false alarms, not all false positives" (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1906.03575, merged from sub-search). TESS catalogs essentially do not calibrate reliability this way: Guerrero et al. 2021 and Kunimoto et al. 2022 use signal-to-pink-noise >= 9 plus vetting with no inverted or scrambled sets; Montalto et al. 2020/2022 use injections for completeness and a classifier with a fixed 1-2.3 % false-positive rate; Feliz et al. 2021 use 21,600 injections and TLS SDE >= 10 (merged from sub-search). Completeness is calibrated by injection (Rodel 2024; LEO-Vetter 91 % completeness / 97 % reliability on simulated data) and reliability, where it is done at all, by vetting-tool performance on known false positives (Giacalone 2021: 68 confirmed/rejected TOIs); Rabtsevich's protocol used 20,000 fake-period trials (DECISIONS item 2), which is a period-scramble rather than a data-scramble. The TLS FAQ's SDE-to-FAP table (7.0 for 1 %, 9.1 for 0.01 %) "assumes Gaussian white noise" (https://transitleastsquares.readthedocs.io/en/latest/FAQ.html, merged from sub-search), so the team should quote the 0.5719-d significance as an empirical rate from its own null population, which it does.

**What the team should change.** (1) Report completeness and reliability as separate numbers: the "~4 false positives expected at SDE >= 14.3" and "0 of 300 above 12" are reliability numbers from an inverted null and should carry the Thompson caveat that inversion under-counts asymmetric systematics; the 38-59 % end-to-end figures from `single_injection.py` are completeness and are the only completeness numbers the project has (the periodic search has known-signal recovery, which is not an injection test). (2) Add a scrambled null for the stitched periodic search: permute the sector or season order while keeping each sector intact, exactly as DR25 did by year. It preserves the asymmetric scattered-light dips that inversion destroys and tests the specific failure mode of section 7.1 (a multi-year lineup selected over 10^6 periods). (3) For the single-transit pass the inverted null is less wrong (single dips vs bumps), but flares invert into dips (REPORT section 10), so a scrambled-time null (shuffle sectors across stars on the same CCD) would give a cleaner single-event false-alarm rate.

**C. Standards for future findings**

* Report completeness (injection) and reliability (inversion + scrambling) separately and never call a periodogram threshold a detection.
* Use the TRICERATOPS thresholds (FPP < 0.01, NFPP < 1e-3, Rp < 8 R_Earth, SNR >= 15) as the definition of "validated" and reserve "confirmed" for RV masses; everything else is "candidate".
* Treat two matched FFI events as a lead, not a candidate (Hawthorn: 88 % false; team: 158 real vs 160 inverted), and require either a third event, a ground-based recovery, or a CHEOPS/TESS re-observation.
* Pre-register on Zenodo before the test data exist, as Rabtsevich did; keep v1 as the test of record when ephemerides are refined.
* Publish through a refereed venue with a professional co-author before any CTOI; join TFOP as a general or observer member so that follow-up requests go through SG1-SG4.
* Keep a per-sector pipeline-provenance note (QLP aperture era S1-S93 vs TGLC era S94+, timing-error sectors, column names) in every candidate record.
